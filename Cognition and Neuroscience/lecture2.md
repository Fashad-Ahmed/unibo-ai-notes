### 1. The Two Major Classes of Cells in the Nervous System
The nervous system is built upon two primary types of cells: **neurons** (the signaling units) and **glial cells** (the support units). Surprisingly, glial cells vastly outnumber neurons by 2 to 10 times in the vertebrate central nervous system (CNS). 

**Glial Cells (Neuroglia) and Their Roles:**
Glial cells provide structural, immune, nourishment, and signaling support. There are four main types discussed:
*   **Microglia (Immune Support):** These are the brain's protectors. They identify when something goes wrong, clear away dead cells, and act as phagocytes during infections or injuries. 
    *   *Scenario:* **Alzheimer's Disease.** In neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer's, microglia can become "hyperactivated." Instead of just protecting the brain, this hyperactivation promotes neuroinflammation, leading to the toxic protein deposits (amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles) that characterize the disease.
*   **Astrocytes (Nourishing Support):** These star-shaped cells make up nearly half of all brain cells. They surround neurons and blood vessels, nourishing neurons, regulating extracellular ions/neurotransmitters, and maintaining the vital blood-brain barrier.
*   **Oligodendrocytes & Schwann Cells (Signaling Support):** Oligodendrocytes operate in the CNS, while Schwann cells operate in the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS). Both wrap thin sheets of fatty **myelin** concentrically around neuronal axons to insulate them, allowing electrical signals to travel incredibly fast. Myelin is what gives "white matter" its name.
    *   *Scenario:* **Multiple Sclerosis (MS).** MS is an autoimmune disease where the body mistakenly attacks its own oligodendrocytes. This damages and scars the myelin sheath in the CNS. Without this insulation, electrical messages traveling along the nerves become severely slowed or disrupted, causing symptoms like numbness, tingling, and motor impairment.

### 2. Neuron Structure and "Dynamic Polarization"
Neurons are the primary signaling units, and there are roughly 100 billion of them in the nervous system. The shape (arborization) of a neuron dictates its ability to interact and compute. Information flows through a neuron in one specific, unidirectional path—a principle known as **dynamic polarization**. 

A neuron has four main components, each with a specific signaling role:
*   **Dendrites (Input Zone):** Branch-like structures that receive incoming signals (inputs) from other nerve cells.
*   **Cell Body / Soma (Integration Zone):** The metabolic center containing the nucleus and endoplasmic reticulum. At the junction of the cell body and the axon (the axon hillock), the neuron "integrates" incoming signals to decide if it will fire.
*   **Axon (Conductive Zone):** A single, long projection that transmits electrical signals away from the cell body. Axons can be anywhere from 0.1 millimeters to 2 meters long. 
*   **Synapses (Output Zone):** Specialized structures at the axon terminals where the neuron passes its chemical or electrical signal to the next cell. 

### 3. Electrical Signaling: From Resting to Firing
Neurons communicate via transient changes in their electrical state, driven by the movement of ions (like sodium and potassium) across the cell membrane. 

*   **The Resting Membrane Potential:** When a neuron is at rest, the inside of the cell is roughly **-70 mV** more negative than the outside. This unequal distribution of ions acts like a stored battery, providing the baseline energy required for all subsequent signaling.
*   **Postsynaptic Potentials (PSPs):** When a signal is received at the dendrites, it creates a PSP. These are **graded potentials**, meaning their size and duration vary depending on the strength of the incoming stimulus. 
    *   *Scenario:* **The 1-Millimeter Limit.** PSPs are passively conducted and suffer from "decremental conduction"—they quickly lose strength and die out over distance. A PSP can only travel a maximum of about 1 millimeter. If a human motor neuron spans from the base of the spine to the toes (over a meter), a simple PSP is far too weak to send a signal that distance.
*   **Integration and Summation:** Because a single excitatory PSP is rarely enough to trigger the neuron to fire, the neuron must add them together at the axon hillock. 
    *   *Spatial Summation:* Adding together inputs arriving simultaneously at *different locations* on the dendrites.
    *   *Temporal Summation:* Adding together inputs arriving at the *same location* but at slightly different times.
    *   *Scenario:* **Reaching the Threshold.** Think of the axon hillock like a voting booth. It tallies all the excitatory (vote "yes") and inhibitory (vote "no") PSPs. Only if the combined depolarization reaches a specific threshold of **-55 mV** will the neuron generate an action potential. Only "meaningful" information that passes this threshold is transmitted.

### 4. The Action Potential (AP): Traveling Far and Fast
If the -55 mV threshold is met, the neuron fires an **Action Potential (AP)**. To overcome the evolutionary challenge of long-distance communication without signal degradation, the AP has unique properties:

*   **Self-Regenerative (Conducted without Decrement):** Unlike PSPs, APs do not weaken. When the threshold is hit, voltage-gated Sodium (Na+) channels open, flooding the cell with positive charge (depolarization). This triggers adjacent channels to open, regenerating the signal down the line. Potassium (K+) channels then open to let positive charge out, returning the cell to its resting state (repolarization/hyperpolarization).
*   **Saltatory Conduction:** To travel quickly, the AP "jumps" down the axon. Because myelin covers most of the axon, the AP is only physically regenerated at the uninsulated gaps called the **Nodes of Ranvier**. 
    *   *Scenario:* **Express Train vs. Local Train.** Imagine saltatory conduction like an express train skipping local stops. Instead of the electrical signal having to open every single door (ion channel) down the entire 1-meter axon, the myelin allows the electrical current to instantly "jump" from one Node of Ranvier to the next, vastly increasing transmission speed.
*   **The Refractory Period:** After firing, the neuron enters a brief period where it is impossible to fire again. This limits the maximum frequency of firing to prevent overstimulation, and critically, ensures the signal only travels in one direction (toward the axon terminals) because the membrane behind the active zone cannot be restimulated.
*   **All-or-None (Binary) Nature & Frequency Coding:** Every AP has the exact same amplitude and duration. A massive stimulus does not create a "bigger" action potential.
    *   *Scenario:* **Translating Intensity into Frequency.** If the amplitude of an AP never changes, how does your brain know the difference between a light touch and a painful pinch? The neuron uses **frequency coding**. A stronger stimulus causes the neuron to fire action potentials *more frequently* (a higher rate of APs), and a longer stimulus causes the train of APs to last for a longer duration. At the output zone, this higher frequency translates directly into the release of more neurotransmitters.
