
## Describe the structure of a neuron and explain how each component contributes to receiving, processing, and transmitting information

### 1. Define the core concept

A **neuron** is a specialized signaling cell of the nervous system. Its structure is adapted to perform three main operations: receive information, integrate or process that information, and transmit an output to another neuron, muscle, or gland. Neurons vary greatly in shape and connectivity, and these structural differences are closely related to their functions. 

### 2. State what will be discussed

The main components of a neuron are the **dendrites, cell body, axon hillock, axon, myelin sheath, and axon terminals**. Together, these form an information-processing sequence from input to integration, conduction, and output.

### 3. Structure and function of each component

**Dendrites** are highly branched extensions of the neuron that form its main receptive or input region. They contain receptors and receive signals at synapses from other neurons. Neurotransmitters released by presynaptic cells produce small graded electrical changes called postsynaptic potentials. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials increase the probability that the neuron will fire, whereas inhibitory postsynaptic potentials reduce it. A large and complex dendritic tree allows a neuron to receive information from many different sources. 

The **cell body, or soma**, contains the nucleus, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, and other organelles. It maintains the neuron by producing proteins and supplying the energy required for signaling. Functionally, the soma also receives synaptic inputs and helps integrate the electrical signals arriving from different dendrites.

The **axon hillock and initial segment** form the principal integrative or trigger region. Excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials spread from the dendrites and soma toward this area. They are combined through **spatial summation**, involving inputs from different synapses, and **temporal summation**, involving inputs arriving close together in time. If the total depolarization reaches threshold, an action potential is generated. 

The **axon** is usually a single elongated process and forms the transmitting region of the neuron. It carries action potentials away from the cell body toward the terminals. Unlike graded postsynaptic potentials, action potentials are all-or-none and self-regenerating, allowing signals to travel without losing strength. Axons vary greatly in length and may transmit information across distances ranging from fractions of a millimetre to approximately two metres. 

Many axons are covered by a **myelin sheath**, formed by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system. Myelin electrically insulates the axon. Gaps called **nodes of Ranvier** contain large numbers of voltage-gated ion channels, allowing the action potential to be regenerated from node to node. This saltatory conduction greatly increases the speed and energy efficiency of transmission.

The axon divides into **axon terminals**, which form the output region. When an action potential reaches a terminal, it opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium entry causes synaptic vesicles to release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The transmitter then binds to receptors on the next cell, producing an excitatory, inhibitory, or modulatory effect. Information is therefore passed from the neuron to another neuron or to an effector such as a muscle or gland. The lecture summarizes this sequence as reception at dendritic synapses, conduction within the neuron, transmission down the axon, and transfer at terminal synapses. 

### 4. Broader point

Although these regions have distinct functions, they operate as one coordinated system. Dendritic shape determines which inputs can be received, the trigger zone determines whether an output is produced, and axonal length and myelination determine how rapidly and efficiently that output reaches its target.

### 5. Conclusion

The structure of a neuron directly supports its signaling function. Dendrites receive information, the soma and axon hillock integrate it, the axon conducts the action potential, and the terminals transmit the output across synapses. Thus, neuronal form is not incidental: it enables the neuron to receive, evaluate, and communicate information efficiently.

## Explain neuronal signal transduction and compare PSPs with action potentials

### 1. Define the core concepts

Neuronal signal transduction is the process by which a neuron receives signals, integrates them, generates an electrical output, and transmits that output to another cell. Two main electrical signals are involved:

* **Postsynaptic potentials (PSPs)** are local, graded changes in membrane potential produced by synaptic input.
* **Action potentials (APs)** are rapid, all-or-none electrical signals that propagate along the axon without decreasing in strength.

Together, PSPs allow the neuron to evaluate incoming information, while APs carry the resulting output over long distances.

### 2. State what will be discussed

The process can be divided into four stages: reception at dendrites, integration at the axon hillock, propagation along the axon, and transmission at the axon terminals. The key distinction is that PSPs are short-distance and graded, whereas APs are long-distance and self-regenerating.

### 3. Signal transduction within the neuron

At rest, the neuron has a negative **resting membrane potential**, maintained by unequal ion concentrations, selective membrane permeability, and the sodium–potassium pump.

Input begins when a presynaptic neuron releases neurotransmitter into a synapse. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron's dendrites or cell body and changes ion flow across the membrane. This produces a PSP.

An **excitatory postsynaptic potential**, or EPSP, depolarizes the membrane and makes an AP more likely. An **inhibitory postsynaptic potential**, or IPSP, hyperpolarizes the membrane or counteracts excitation, making an AP less likely.

PSPs are graded: their size depends on the strength of the input. However, they are passively conducted through the dendrites and soma and decrease in amplitude with distance. The lectures note that PSPs act over approximately one millimetre, so they cannot carry information along the entire axon.  

Because a single EPSP is generally insufficient to trigger firing, the neuron combines many excitatory and inhibitory inputs at the **axon hillock or initial segment**. In **spatial summation**, PSPs from different synapses are combined. In **temporal summation**, PSPs arriving close together in time overlap. The neuron therefore evaluates the total pattern of incoming excitation and inhibition. 

If the summed depolarization reaches threshold, an AP is initiated. Voltage-gated sodium channels open, causing rapid sodium entry and depolarization. Sodium channels then inactivate while voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium leaves the cell, causing repolarization and a brief after-hyperpolarization.

The AP is **all-or-none**: once threshold is crossed, its amplitude and duration are relatively constant. Stronger inputs do not produce larger APs. Instead, greater PSP amplitude or duration produces a higher frequency or longer train of APs. Thus, graded synaptic input is converted into a frequency code at the trigger zone. 

The AP travels along the axon because depolarization in one segment opens voltage-gated channels in the next. This makes the signal self-regenerating rather than decremental. Refractory periods prevent immediate reactivation of the previous segment and help ensure forward propagation.

In myelinated axons, myelin electrically insulates the membrane. APs are regenerated mainly at the nodes of Ranvier, producing **saltatory conduction**. This greatly increases conduction speed and reduces energy use.

When the AP reaches the presynaptic terminal, it opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium enters and causes synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane. Neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic cleft, diffuses across it, and binds to receptors on the next cell, beginning a new PSP. The presynaptic neuron is therefore the transmitting cell, while the postsynaptic cell receives the signal.  

### 4. PSPs compared with APs

| Characteristic     | Postsynaptic potentials              | Action potentials                               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Main location      | Dendrites and soma                   | Axon initial segment and axon                   |
| Function           | Receive and integrate input          | Carry neuronal output                           |
| Amplitude          | Graded; depends on input strength    | All-or-none; relatively constant                |
| Types              | Excitatory or inhibitory             | No separate excitatory/inhibitory forms         |
| Conduction         | Passive and decremental              | Active and self-regenerating                    |
| Distance           | Short, local                         | Long-distance                                   |
| Summation          | Can summate spatially and temporally | Do not summate                                  |
| Information coding | Amplitude and duration               | Frequency, timing, and duration of spike trains |

### 5. Conclusion

PSPs and APs perform complementary functions. PSPs allow the neuron to receive and integrate many excitatory and inhibitory inputs. When their combined effect reaches threshold at the axon hillock, they generate an AP. The AP then propagates without losing amplitude, allowing reliable long-distance communication. At the terminal, it is converted into neurotransmitter release, which transfers information to the next cell.

## Discuss how the four properties of action potentials enable efficient long-distance communication

### 1. Define the core concept

An **action potential (AP)** is a rapid electrical signal that travels along a neuron’s axon. Unlike postsynaptic potentials, which weaken as they spread, an AP is actively regenerated and can therefore communicate information over long distances. The four key properties are **threshold for initiation, conduction without decrement, the refractory period, and the all-or-none nature**. 

### 2. State the argument

These properties make neuronal communication reliable, directional, rapid, and resistant to signal loss. Together, they allow a small region of membrane at the axon hillock to generate a signal that can reach distant axon terminals with essentially the same strength.

### 3. The four properties

#### 1. Threshold for initiation

An AP begins only when depolarization at the axon hillock reaches a critical **threshold**. Below threshold, no AP occurs; once threshold is reached, voltage-gated sodium channels open rapidly.

This property ensures that the neuron does not transmit an output in response to every weak or random fluctuation in membrane potential. Instead, it integrates excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials and fires only when their net effect is sufficiently strong. Threshold therefore acts as a decision mechanism and improves the reliability of neural signaling.

#### 2. Conducted without decrement

Once initiated, the AP is **actively propagated and self-regenerating**. Sodium entering one part of the axon depolarizes the adjacent membrane, causing voltage-gated sodium channels there to open. The process is repeated along the entire axon. 

Because each section of membrane generates a new AP, the signal does not gradually weaken with distance. This is essential because some axons extend over very long distances. A signal generated near the spinal cord, for example, must still be strong enough to activate terminals in a distant muscle.

In myelinated axons, current travels rapidly beneath the myelin sheath and APs are regenerated mainly at the nodes of Ranvier. This saltatory conduction further increases speed and energy efficiency.

#### 3. Refractory period

After an AP, the membrane temporarily becomes less excitable. During the **absolute refractory period**, sodium channels are inactivated and another AP cannot occur. During the **relative refractory period**, another AP is possible but requires stronger stimulation.

The refractory period has two important consequences. First, it limits the maximum firing rate and prevents excessive or continuous excitation. Second, it promotes **unidirectional propagation**. The membrane immediately behind the advancing AP is refractory and cannot be reactivated, whereas the membrane ahead remains excitable. The signal therefore normally travels from the axon hillock toward the terminals. 

#### 4. All-or-none nature

An AP either occurs fully or does not occur at all. Once threshold is reached, its amplitude and general form remain largely constant, regardless of how strong the initiating stimulus was.

This makes long-distance signaling reliable because weak and strong stimuli do not produce APs of progressively different sizes that could become difficult to distinguish after traveling along the axon. Instead, stimulus intensity is encoded through the **frequency, number, and timing of APs**. A stronger stimulus normally produces a higher firing rate or a longer train of APs, not larger individual APs.

### 4. Conclusion

The four properties of APs work together to support efficient long-distance communication. Threshold prevents inappropriate firing, active regeneration prevents signal loss, refractory periods control firing rate and maintain direction, and all-or-none signaling guarantees a consistent output. As a result, neurons can transmit precise and reliable information from the axon hillock to distant synaptic terminals.

### Explain the significance of the "all-or-none" nature of action potentials. How
do neurons communicate information about the strength or intensity of a
stimulus given this limitation?

The **all-or-none principle** means that an action potential is always generated at the same size and amplitude once the membrane potential reaches the threshold. If the threshold is not reached, **no action potential occurs**. There are no "strong" or "weak" action potentials—they are either fully produced or not produced at all.

### Significance of the All-or-None Principle

* **Reliable signal transmission:** Action potentials maintain their amplitude as they travel along the axon, ensuring information is transmitted accurately over long distances.
* **Prevents signal degradation:** Since each action potential is regenerated along the axon, the signal does not weaken with distance.
* **Consistent communication:** Target cells receive standardized electrical signals, reducing errors in neural communication.

### How Neurons Encode Stimulus Intensity

Although individual action potentials do not vary in size, neurons represent the strength of a stimulus using other mechanisms:

1. **Frequency Coding (Rate Coding)**

   * Stronger stimuli produce **more frequent action potentials**.
   * Weaker stimuli generate action potentials at a lower frequency.
   * Example: Touching a hot surface causes sensory neurons to fire much more rapidly than touching a warm object.

2. **Population Coding (Recruitment)**

   * As stimulus intensity increases, **more neurons are activated**.
   * The brain interprets a stronger stimulus from the combined activity of a larger population of neurons.
   * Example: A bright light activates more photoreceptors than a dim light.

3. **Temporal Patterns**

   * The timing and pattern of action potentials can also convey information about the characteristics of a stimulus, such as its duration or rate of change.

### Summary

The all-or-none nature of action potentials ensures fast and reliable transmission of nerve impulses. Neurons overcome the inability to vary the size of an action potential by encoding stimulus intensity through **the frequency of action potentials, the number of neurons recruited, and the timing of their firing**, allowing the nervous system to distinguish between weak and strong stimuli.

### Discuss the significance of myelin in neuronal signaling. Explain how myelin contributes to the speed and efficiency of action potential propagation, and what happens when myelin is damaged in diseases like Multiple Sclerosis.

**Myelin** is a fatty, insulating sheath that surrounds the axons of many neurons. It is produced by **Schwann cells** in the peripheral nervous system (PNS) and **oligodendrocytes** in the central nervous system (CNS). Myelin plays a crucial role in ensuring that nerve impulses are transmitted rapidly and efficiently.

### Role of Myelin in Neuronal Signaling

1. **Increases Conduction Speed**

   * Myelin prevents the leakage of electrical current across the axon membrane.
   * Action potentials are generated only at the **Nodes of Ranvier**, which are small gaps between myelinated segments.
   * The electrical impulse appears to "jump" from one node to the next, a process called **saltatory conduction**.
   * This allows signals to travel much faster than in unmyelinated axons.

2. **Improves Energy Efficiency**

   * Because ion exchange occurs only at the Nodes of Ranvier, fewer sodium-potassium pumps are needed to restore ion balance.
   * As a result, neurons use **less ATP (energy)** to maintain and restore the resting membrane potential after an action potential.

3. **Enhances Reliable Signal Transmission**

   * Myelin reduces the loss of electrical current along the axon.
   * This ensures that action potentials travel long distances without weakening, allowing rapid and accurate communication between different parts of the nervous system.

### What Happens When Myelin Is Damaged?

In **Multiple Sclerosis (MS)**, the immune system mistakenly attacks the myelin sheath in the central nervous system, causing **demyelination**.

The consequences include:

* **Slower nerve impulse conduction** because saltatory conduction is disrupted.
* **Electrical signal leakage**, making it difficult for action potentials to reach the next Node of Ranvier.
* **Conduction block**, where nerve impulses fail to propagate altogether in severely damaged areas.

### Symptoms of Multiple Sclerosis

The disruption of nerve signaling can lead to a variety of neurological symptoms, including:

* Muscle weakness or paralysis
* Numbness and tingling sensations
* Poor coordination and balance
* Vision problems (such as blurred or double vision)
* Fatigue
* Difficulty with speech, memory, and concentration

### Conclusion

Myelin is essential for the **speed, efficiency, and reliability** of neuronal communication. By enabling **saltatory conduction**, it allows action potentials to travel rapidly while conserving energy. Damage to myelin, as seen in **Multiple Sclerosis**, disrupts this process, slowing or blocking nerve impulse transmission and leading to a wide range of neurological impairments.

## Compare and contrast electrical and chemical synapses, focusing on their structural differences, mechanisms of signal transmission, and functional implications for neural communication.

### 1. Define the core concept

A **synapse** is a specialized junction through which a presynaptic neuron communicates with a postsynaptic cell. Synapses may be **electrical**, where ionic current passes directly between cells, or **chemical**, where neurotransmitters carry the signal across a synaptic cleft. Both allow communication between neurons, but their structures and functional properties are very different. 

### 2. Structural differences

In an **electrical synapse**, the membranes of two cells are connected by **gap junctions**. These junctions contain channels that directly link the cytoplasm of the two cells, allowing ions and small molecules to pass between them. The cells are therefore separated by only a very narrow gap.

In a **chemical synapse**, the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes are separated by a wider **synaptic cleft**. The presynaptic terminal contains synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter, while the postsynaptic membrane contains specific receptors. The two cells are not physically continuous.

### 3. Mechanisms of signal transmission

At an electrical synapse, a change in membrane potential in one neuron produces ionic current that flows directly through gap junctions into the other cell. Transmission is therefore extremely rapid and has almost no synaptic delay. It is often bidirectional, although some electrical synapses show preferential transmission in one direction.

At a chemical synapse, an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal and opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium enters the terminal and triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane. Neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic cleft, diffuses across it, and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic cell. Receptor activation then produces an excitatory, inhibitory, or modulatory postsynaptic response. Chemical transmission is usually unidirectional because transmitter release occurs presynaptically and receptors are concentrated postsynaptically.

### 4. Functional implications

Electrical synapses are especially useful when speed and synchronization are required. Because current passes directly from cell to cell, groups of neurons can fire together with very little delay. This makes electrical synapses suitable for rapid reflexes, rhythmic activity, and synchronized neural networks. However, they offer relatively limited amplification, flexibility, and modulation.

Chemical synapses are slower because transmission involves several stages, including calcium entry, vesicle release, diffusion, and receptor activation. However, they are much more flexible. Depending on the transmitter and receptor type, a chemical synapse can excite, inhibit, or modulate the postsynaptic cell. Chemical synapses also permit amplification, integration of many inputs, and substantial plasticity. Changes in neurotransmitter release, receptor number, receptor sensitivity, or synaptic structure can strengthen or weaken communication, making chemical synapses especially important for learning and memory.

Chemical synapses also allow greater specificity. One neuron can communicate differently with different target cells because each postsynaptic cell may express different receptor types. By contrast, electrical synapses transmit the presynaptic voltage more directly and provide less opportunity for signal transformation.

### 5. Comparison table

| Characteristic        | Electrical synapse                               | Chemical synapse                                       |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Structural connection | Gap junctions directly connect cells             | Cells separated by synaptic cleft                      |
| Signal carrier        | Direct ionic current                             | Neurotransmitter                                       |
| Speed                 | Extremely fast                                   | Slower                                                 |
| Synaptic delay        | Minimal                                          | Present                                                |
| Direction             | Often bidirectional                              | Usually unidirectional                                 |
| Excitation/inhibition | Usually preserves the sign of the voltage change | Can be excitatory, inhibitory, or modulatory           |
| Signal modification   | Limited                                          | Extensive                                              |
| Plasticity            | Relatively limited                               | High                                                   |
| Main function         | Synchronization and rapid responses              | Flexible processing, integration, learning, and memory |

### 6. Conclusion

Electrical and chemical synapses are complementary. Electrical synapses prioritize **speed, reliability, and synchronization**, while chemical synapses prioritize **flexibility, specificity, modulation, and plasticity**. Electrical transmission is therefore well suited to rapid coordinated activity, whereas chemical transmission supports the complex information processing required for most sensory, motor, and cognitive functions.

### Describe the components of a neural circuit, including sensory neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons. Provide an example of a simple neural circuit, such as the knee-jerk reflex, and explain how it functions.



## Describe the components of a neural circuit and explain the knee-jerk reflex

### 1. Define the core concept

A **neural circuit** is a group of interconnected neurons that receives information, processes it, and produces a response. Most circuits contain three main functional components: **sensory neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons**.

### 2. Components of a neural circuit

**Sensory neurons**, also called afferent neurons, detect changes inside or outside the body. Their receptors convert stimuli such as pressure, light, temperature, or muscle stretch into electrical signals. These signals travel toward the central nervous system.

**Interneurons** are located mainly within the brain and spinal cord. They connect neurons to one another and process incoming information. Interneurons may be excitatory or inhibitory, allowing them to increase or reduce the activity of other neurons. They make circuits more flexible by integrating information, coordinating different pathways, and selecting an appropriate response.

**Motor neurons**, also called efferent neurons, carry commands from the central nervous system to effectors such as muscles or glands. When a motor neuron activates a skeletal muscle, neurotransmitter release at the neuromuscular junction causes the muscle to contract.

The general flow of information is therefore:

**sensory input → central processing → motor output**

However, circuits may contain parallel pathways, feedback loops, and both excitatory and inhibitory connections.

### 3. The knee-jerk reflex

The **knee-jerk, or patellar, reflex** is a simple spinal circuit that helps maintain posture and muscle length.

When a reflex hammer taps the patellar tendon below the kneecap, the tendon pulls on and briefly stretches the quadriceps muscle. Stretch-sensitive receptors called **muscle spindles** detect this change. The sensory neuron connected to the muscle spindle generates action potentials and carries the information into the spinal cord. 

Inside the spinal cord, the sensory neuron divides into two functional pathways.

First, it forms a direct **excitatory synapse** with motor neurons controlling the quadriceps. These motor neurons send action potentials back to the muscle, causing it to contract and extend the lower leg. Because this part contains only one synapse between the sensory and motor neurons, it is described as a **monosynaptic reflex**.

Second, the sensory neuron activates an **inhibitory interneuron**. This interneuron inhibits motor neurons supplying the opposing hamstring muscles. The hamstrings therefore relax while the quadriceps contracts. This process is called **reciprocal inhibition**. It prevents the antagonist muscle from resisting the movement. 

Thus, the complete response depends on both excitation and inhibition:

* Quadriceps motor neurons are excited.
* Hamstring motor neurons are inhibited.
* The lower leg extends rapidly and smoothly.

### 4. Functional significance

The reflex is fast because the essential processing occurs in the spinal cord rather than waiting for conscious cortical control. Nevertheless, sensory information also travels upward to the brain, allowing the person to become aware of the stimulus after the reflex has begun.

The reflex also demonstrates how circuit structure determines function. A direct sensory-to-motor connection produces a rapid contraction, while the additional interneuron pathway coordinates the opposing muscle. Although diagrams often show single neurons, each motor-neuron symbol represents a population of cells working together. 

### 5. Conclusion

A neural circuit transforms sensory information into an appropriate response. Sensory neurons detect and carry input, interneurons integrate and modify it, and motor neurons activate effectors. In the knee-jerk reflex, excitation of the quadriceps and interneuron-mediated inhibition of the hamstrings produce rapid extension of the lower leg.

### Discuss the organization (direct and indirect pathways) and role of the basal ganglia in motor control, reinforcement learning, and goal-oriented behavior.

The basal ganglia are a pair of subcortical brain areas which have a crucial role in motor control,
reinforcement learning, and goal-oriented behavior. This is because basal ganglia are involved
in one of the main dopaminergic pathways in the nervous system. Dopamine quantities, as the
brain’s encoding of reward prediction errors, crucially enable reinforcement learning.
The primary role of the basal ganglia is to receive most sensory input from the spinal cord and
transfer it to the thalamus, which can be done via either the direct or indirect pathway. Which
pathway is used is controlled by a part of the basal ganglia called the substantia nigra pars
compacta (SNc), and the striatum. The SNc releases quantities of dopamine in response to
certain stimuli, which acts upon the striatum like a switch; the striatum then, in turn, controls
which pathway the stimulus will travel through to the thalamus, depending on the amount of
dopamine released:
- The shorter, direct pathway, disinhibits the thalamus and stimulates movement.
- The longer, indirect pathway, inhibits the thalamus instead.
This mechanism ensures that rewarding actions and stimuli are reinforced by traveling through
the shorter, more direct pathway.
In certain diseases, such as Parkinson’s, a loss of dopaminergic neurons can cause reduced or
difficult mobility - but also compulsive shopping, gambling, or other dopamine (reward prediction
error)-seeking activities.




Basic organization

The striatum is the main input structure of the basal ganglia. It receives excitatory input from large areas of the cerebral cortex. The principal output structures are the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr).

GPi and SNr normally provide continuous inhibitory output to the thalamus. The thalamus, in turn, sends excitatory signals back to the cortex. Movement therefore depends partly on whether basal-ganglia pathways reduce or strengthen this inhibition of the thalamus.

The direct pathway

The direct pathway facilitates a selected action:

Cortex → striatum → GPi/SNr → thalamus → cortex

The cortex excites neurons in the striatum.
The striatum inhibits the GPi and SNr.
Because GPi and SNr normally inhibit the thalamus, inhibiting them removes this brake.
The thalamus is therefore disinhibited.
The thalamus excites the motor cortex more strongly, helping initiate or facilitate the selected movement.

The direct pathway can therefore be summarized as inhibition of an inhibitor, resulting in increased cortical activity and promotion of action.


The indirect pathway

The indirect pathway suppresses competing actions:

Cortex → striatum → GPe → subthalamic nucleus → GPi/SNr → thalamus → cortex

The cortex excites the striatum.
The striatum inhibits the external segment of the globus pallidus, or GPe.
The GPe normally inhibits the subthalamic nucleus, so inhibition of the GPe disinhibits the subthalamic nucleus.
The subthalamic nucleus excites the GPi and SNr.
GPi and SNr increase their inhibition of the thalamus.
Thalamic excitation of the cortex decreases, suppressing movement.

Thus, the indirect pathway reinforces the normal inhibition of the thalamus and reduces the likelihood of an action being performed.

The two pathways should not be viewed simply as an on-switch and an off-switch for all movement. During normal behavior, the direct pathway helps release the desired action, while the indirect pathway suppresses alternative actions that would interfere with it.



Dopamine and pathway balance

The substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) sends dopaminergic projections to the striatum. Dopamine generally:

activates direct-pathway neurons through D1 receptors;
inhibits indirect-pathway neurons through D2 receptors.

Both effects favor the selected action: dopamine strengthens the pathway that facilitates it and weakens the pathway that suppresses it. The SNc therefore helps regulate the balance between direct and indirect pathways.


Role in motor control

The basal ganglia help select, initiate, scale, and terminate movements. They allow one motor program to proceed while inhibiting competing programs. They are particularly important for smooth voluntary movement, learned action sequences, and automatic skills.

The role of dopamine is illustrated by Parkinson’s disease. Loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SNc reduces direct-pathway activity and increases the relative influence of the indirect pathway. This produces excessive thalamic inhibition and reduced cortical motor activation, contributing to hypokinesia, bradykinesia, and difficulty initiating movement.

Reinforcement learning

The basal ganglia also connect rewards to actions. Dopamine release reflects, at least partly, a reward-prediction error: the difference between expected and actual reward.

An unexpectedly good outcome produces increased dopamine signaling.
A fully predicted reward produces less change.
An outcome worse than expected produces reduced dopamine activity.

These signals modify synaptic plasticity in the striatum. Cortical inputs associated with rewarded actions are strengthened, making those actions more likely to be selected again. The striatum can therefore act as an interface through which reward influences action selection.

Goal-oriented behavior

In goal-directed behavior, the basal ganglia integrate information about possible actions, expected outcomes, motivation, and reward value. Cortico-basal-ganglia loops involving prefrontal and associative regions help select behaviors that are likely to achieve current goals.

With repetition, actions may become less dependent on conscious evaluation and more habitual. Therefore, the basal ganglia contribute to both:

goal-directed actions, selected according to expected consequences;
habits, triggered more automatically by familiar contexts.

Abnormal reward-related dopamine signaling can also contribute to maladaptive behaviors such as pathological gambling or compulsive shopping.



Conclusion

The basal ganglia regulate behavior through opposing but coordinated pathways. The direct pathway disinhibits the thalamus and facilitates selected actions, while the indirect pathway increases thalamic inhibition and suppresses competing actions. Dopamine from the SNc shifts the balance toward rewarded actions and modifies striatal plasticity through reward-prediction signals.

The basal ganglia therefore form a common action-selection system linking motor control, reinforcement learning, habits, and goal-oriented behavior.

## Describe the organization of the cerebral cortex, including its lobes and their key functions

### 1. Define the cerebral cortex

The **cerebral cortex** is the outer layer of the cerebral hemispheres. It is composed mainly of gray matter containing neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, and glial cells. Beneath it lies white matter, which contains myelinated axons connecting different cortical and subcortical regions.

The cortex supports higher cognitive functions, including perception, voluntary movement, language, memory, attention, planning, and decision-making. 

### 2. General organization

The cerebral cortex is divided into **two approximately symmetrical hemispheres**, left and right, connected mainly by the **corpus callosum**. This large white-matter pathway allows information to pass between the hemispheres.

Each hemisphere is divided into four main lobes by prominent grooves, or **sulci**:

* frontal lobe;
* parietal lobe;
* temporal lobe;
* occipital lobe.

The cortical surface is folded into raised ridges called **gyri** and grooves called sulci. Folding increases the cortical surface area, allowing more neurons to fit within the skull.

Although each lobe has characteristic functions, cognition does not normally depend on one isolated area. Most cognitive systems consist of distributed networks whose components are located in several lobes. Cortical systems are also often hierarchically organized into primary, secondary, and tertiary association areas. 

**Primary areas** perform the earliest cortical processing of sensory information or directly control movement. **Secondary areas** process more complex features within the same sensory or motor domain. **Tertiary association areas** integrate information across different modalities and contribute to higher cognition.

### 3. Frontal lobe

The frontal lobe lies at the front of the brain and is involved in **movement, planning, cognitive control, decision-making, motivation, and personality**. 

The **primary motor cortex**, located in the precentral gyrus, contributes to the execution of voluntary movement. It contains a somatotopic representation of the body, often called the motor homunculus. Different cortical regions control different body parts, and body parts requiring fine control occupy more cortical space.

Premotor and supplementary motor areas contribute to movement preparation, sequencing, and coordination.

The **prefrontal cortex** supports executive functions, including working memory, inhibition, long-term planning, organizing behavior, evaluating consequences, and selecting actions according to goals. Ventromedial and orbitofrontal regions are especially important for integrating emotional and reward information into decisions. 

### 4. Parietal lobe

The parietal lobe lies behind the frontal lobe. Its anterior region contains the **primary somatosensory cortex**, which receives information about touch, pain, temperature, and the position of the limbs.

This cortex is also somatotopically organized. Body regions with high sensory sensitivity, such as the hands and lips, occupy disproportionately large cortical areas.

Posterior parietal regions integrate sensory information and contribute to:

* spatial attention and awareness;
* representation of the body and surrounding space;
* coordination of vision and action;
* reaching, grasping, and other visually guided movements.

Damage, particularly to the right parietal cortex, can produce hemispatial neglect, in which the patient fails to attend to the left side of space despite relatively intact basic vision. The lecture therefore describes the parietal lobe as important for sensory processing, spatial coding, and action coordination. 

### 5. Temporal lobe

The temporal lobe lies on the side of the brain below the lateral sulcus. It contains **auditory, visual, and multimodal processing areas**. 

The primary auditory cortex processes basic properties of sound. Surrounding temporal regions contribute to speech and language comprehension. In most people, left posterior temporal regions are particularly important for understanding language.

The inferior temporal cortex contributes to complex visual recognition, including the identification of objects and faces. Medial temporal structures, especially the hippocampus, are important for the formation of declarative memories. The temporal lobe is therefore involved in hearing, language comprehension, object recognition, memory, and aspects of emotion.

### 6. Occipital lobe

The occipital lobe lies at the back of the brain and is primarily responsible for **visual processing**. 

The primary visual cortex receives organized visual input from the thalamus and contains a retinotopic map of the visual field. Nearby visual association areas analyse increasingly complex properties such as orientation, colour, motion, shape, and object structure.

Visual information is then distributed into broader processing pathways. A dorsal pathway toward parietal cortex contributes to spatial processing and visually guided action, while a ventral pathway toward temporal cortex contributes to object identification.

### 7. Conclusion

The cerebral cortex consists of two interconnected hemispheres divided into frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes. The frontal lobe is especially important for movement and executive control; the parietal lobe for somatosensation, spatial processing, and action; the temporal lobe for hearing, language, recognition, and memory; and the occipital lobe for vision.

However, these divisions are not absolute. Complex cognition emerges from hierarchically organized and widely distributed cortical networks that communicate through white-matter pathways.
