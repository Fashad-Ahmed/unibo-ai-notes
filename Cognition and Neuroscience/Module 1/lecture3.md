### **1. The Structure of Neurons**

The nervous system is built primarily from two classes of cells: **neurons** (which transmit information) and **glial cells** (which provide support and modulation).

Neurons have unique structures designed to receive, process, and transmit electrical and chemical signals:

* **Soma (Cell Body):** Contains the metabolic machinery of the cell (nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum). It is suspended in a salty intracellular fluid called cytoplasm.
* **Dendrites:** Branching extensions (like tree branches) that receive inputs from other neurons. They often have small knobs called *spines* where synapses occur.
* **Axon:** A single, long extension that transmits electrical signals (action potentials) away from the cell body toward other neurons.
* **Myelin:** A fatty sheath wrapping many axons, providing electrical insulation and speeding up signal transmission.
* **Nodes of Ranvier:** Unmyelinated gaps along the axon where the signal is regenerated.


* **Synapse:** The specialized structure at the end of the axon terminal where the neuron communicates with the next cell.

---

### **2. Neuronal Signaling**

Neurons communicate using electrochemical energy. A neuron at rest acts like a tiny battery.

**The Resting Membrane Potential:**

* When not actively firing, the inside of a neuron is negatively charged relative to the outside (typically **-70 millivolts**).
* This voltage difference is maintained by the lipid bilayer membrane, which is selectively permeable.
* **Ion Channels:** Transmembrane proteins that allow specific ions (like Potassium $K^+$, Sodium $Na^+$, or Chloride $Cl^-$) to pass through. Some are passive (always open), and some are "gated" (opening only under specific electrical or chemical conditions).
* **Ion Pumps:** Because the concentration of ions naturally wants to equalize, the neuron uses energy (ATP) to actively pump ions against their gradient. The **$Na^+/K^+$ pump** constantly pushes 3 $Na^+$ out of the cell and pulls 2 $K^+$ into the cell, preserving the negative resting potential.

**The Action Potential:**
Passive currents (from synapses on dendrites) weaken as they travel (decremental conduction). To send a signal over long distances (like from your spinal cord to your toe), the neuron uses an **action potential**—a self-regenerating electrical signal.

1. **Threshold:** If the passive currents depolarize the axon hillock enough (bringing it from -70mV to roughly -55mV), voltage-gated $Na^+$ channels swing open.
2. **Depolarization:** $Na^+$ rushes into the cell, making the inside briefly positive.
3. **Repolarization:** The $Na^+$ channels close, and voltage-gated $K^+$ channels open. $K^+$ rushes out, driving the voltage back down.
4. **Hyperpolarization & Refractory Period:** The voltage briefly drops below the resting potential. During this refractory period, the neuron cannot fire another action potential, which forces the signal to travel in only one direction down the axon.
5. **Saltatory Conduction:** In myelinated axons, the action potential "jumps" from one Node of Ranvier to the next, massively increasing the speed of the signal.

*Key Rule:* Action potentials are **all-or-none**. Stimulus intensity is not communicated by the size of the spike, but by the *rate of firing* (more spikes per second = a stronger signal).

### **3. Synaptic Transmission**

Once the action potential reaches the axon terminal, the signal must cross to the next cell.

**Chemical Synapses (The Most Common):**

1. The action potential depolarizes the terminal, opening voltage-gated $Ca^{2+}$ channels.
2. $Ca^{2+}$ rushes in, causing vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release **neurotransmitters** into the synaptic cleft.
3. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the gap and bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron.
4. This binding opens ion channels on the new cell, causing either excitation (depolarization) or inhibition (hyperpolarization).
*Note: The effect (excitatory or inhibitory) depends on the specific postsynaptic receptor, not just the transmitter itself.*

**Electrical Synapses:**

* There is no synaptic cleft. The cells physically touch at **gap junctions**, allowing ions to flow directly between the cytoplasms of the two cells.
* This transmission is nearly instantaneous and is useful for fast reflexes or synchronizing groups of neurons, but it lacks the plasticity and complex modulation of chemical synapses.

### **4. The Role of Glial Cells**

Glial cells equal or outnumber neurons and provide critical support:

* **Astrocytes:** Star-shaped cells that help form the blood-brain barrier (protecting the brain from blood-borne toxins) and regulate neurotransmitter reuptake, thus modulating neural activity.
* **Microglia:** The brain's immune system; they devour and remove damaged cells.
* **Oligodendrocytes (CNS) & Schwann cells (PNS):** These cells create the myelin sheaths that wrap around axons to speed up electrical conduction.

---

### **5. Overview of Nervous System Structure**

The nervous system is split into two broad categories:

* **Central Nervous System (CNS):** The brain and spinal cord. It is the command-and-control center.
* **Peripheral Nervous System (PNS):** The sensory and motor nerves outside the CNS. It acts as a courier network.
* The PNS includes the **Autonomic Nervous System**, which controls involuntary functions like heart rate and digestion. It is split into the **Sympathetic** (fight or flight) and **Parasympathetic** (rest and digest) branches.

### **6. A Guided Tour of the Brain**

The brain is built from the bottom up, with older, life-sustaining structures at the base, and newer, complex cognitive structures at the top.

**The Brainstem (Medulla, Pons, Midbrain, Cerebellum):**

* **Medulla:** Controls vital reflexes like breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure.
* **Pons:** A bridge to the cerebellum; involved in eye movements, facial motor control, and generating REM sleep.
* **Cerebellum ("little brain"):** Contains the vast majority of the brain's neurons. It doesn't initiate movement, but it integrates sensory and motor commands to ensure movements are smooth, coordinated, and balanced.
* **Midbrain:** Contains structures like the superior and inferior colliculi, which help orient gaze and attention toward visual and auditory stimuli.

**The Diencephalon (Thalamus & Hypothalamus):**

* **Thalamus:** The "Grand Central Station" of the brain. Almost all sensory information (except smell) stops here to be organized and relayed to the cerebral cortex.
* **Hypothalamus:** The main link between the nervous system and the endocrine (hormone) system. It regulates homeostasis, controlling thirst, hunger, temperature, and circadian rhythms, largely by controlling the pituitary gland.

**The Telencephalon (Limbic System, Basal Ganglia, & Cortex):**

* **Limbic System:** A nebulous border region (including the amygdala, hippocampus, and cingulate gyrus) heavily involved in emotion, learning, and memory.
* **Basal Ganglia:** Deep brain nuclei crucial for action selection, reward-based learning, and motor preparation (heavily reliant on dopamine).

### **7. The Cerebral Cortex**

The cerebral cortex is the folded outer layer of the brain, composed of gray matter (cell bodies). The folds consist of **gyri** (the crowns) and **sulci** (the crevices), which massively increase the surface area packed inside the skull.

The cortex is divided anatomically into four lobes:

* **Frontal Lobe:** Contains the motor cortex (for executing movement) and the prefrontal cortex (the center of "executive function"—planning, organizing, decision-making, and inhibiting inappropriate behavior).
* **Parietal Lobe:** Contains the somatosensory cortex. It integrates sensory information about touch, pain, temperature, and spatial location. It features **somatotopic mapping** (the homunculus), where adjacent parts of the body are represented in adjacent areas of the cortex.
* **Occipital Lobe:** Dedicated entirely to visual processing, beginning in the primary visual cortex (V1) where features like edges and light are mapped.
* **Temporal Lobe:** Houses the primary auditory cortex (which has a tonotopic map organized by sound frequency). It also contains advanced visual association areas necessary for object and face recognition.

**Association Cortex:** Any area that is not strictly primary sensory or motor cortex. These areas integrate information across modalities to produce higher-level cognitive functions, language, and abstract thought.

### **8. Development of the Nervous System**

* **Prenatal Development:** The nervous system starts as a simple neural tube. Cells divide in the ventricular zone and migrate outward along "radial glial cells" to form the layers of the cortex. The cortex is built "inside-out"—the deepest layers are formed first.
* **The Big Brain Theory vs. Reality:** Humans don't have magically different brains; we just have a proportionately scaled-up primate brain with massive numbers of neurons (around 86 billion). To stay efficient and fit in the skull, our brains use "small-world architecture"—lots of fast, short, local connections, combined with a few long-distance connections.
* **Adult Neurogenesis:** It was long believed that adults never grow new neurons. We now know this is false; neurogenesis occurs in the adult hippocampus (crucial for learning) and the olfactory bulb.
* **The Baby Brain:** At birth, a baby has most of its neurons, but the brain quadruples in size due to **synaptogenesis** (the explosive growth of new synapses) and myelination. This is followed by years of **pruning** (synapse elimination), fine-tuning the brain's circuits based on experience.
